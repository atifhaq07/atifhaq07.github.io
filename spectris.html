Did KKR overpay for Spectris?

KKR paid £41.75 a share, £4.8bn including debt, for the UK maker of precision measurement instruments after a bidding war with Advent. I rebuilt the deal from public filings to test whether the price can still earn a private equity return.

My answer

On my base case, KKR earns about 11.5% a year (1.72x its money) over five years. That is well short of the roughly 20% private equity firms usually target. To reach 20%, KKR needs to sell at 24.4x EBITDA, or grow 2030 EBITDA about 39% beyond my plan. The price only makes sense if Spectris is worth what US instrument companies trade at.

The deal

Spectris sells high-precision measurement instruments and software to labs and engineering teams. Its shares sat at £20.38 before the bids became public, about 11.2x its 2024 adjusted EBITDA of £239m. Two private equity firms then bid against each other for two months.

  1. 6 Jun 2025Undisturbed share price: £20.38
  2. 23 Jun 2025Advent offers £37.63 (EV £4.4bn), recommended by the board
  3. 2 Jul 2025KKR offers £40.00 (EV £4.7bn); the board switches to KKR
  4. 1 Aug 2025Advent raises to £41.00 (EV £4.8bn)
  5. 5 Aug 2025KKR raises to £41.75 (EV £4.8bn, 20.3x 2024 EBITDA); Advent walks away
  6. 4 Dec 2025Deal completes; Spectris leaves the stock market

KKR funded the deal with a £1.5bn term loan and about £3.5bn of equity from its funds and co-investors. That is only 5.1x net debt to 2025 EBITDA, so roughly 70% of the price was paid in equity. Low leverage matters for the returns below.

What was Spectris worth?

I valued Spectris four ways and lined the results up against the four offers. A banker calls this a football field.

Every standalone measure sits below what KKR paid. My DCF (WACC 8.9%, 2.5% long-run growth) gives £2.89bn, close to the pre-bid market value. UK peers trade at a median 15.8x EBITDA. Only US instrument peers, at a median 22.6x, support the final price.

Can KKR still make money?

I built an integrated three-statement model (income statement, balance sheet and cash flow, all linked) and ran it from completion to the end of 2030. Base case: revenue grows 5% a year, margins rise from 17.1% to 19.5% as Spectris's announced £50m cost savings land, the loan costs 6.5%, and KKR sells at the same multiple it paid.

Equity of £3.48bn grows to £5.99bn. About 78% of the gain comes from growing EBITDA and 22% from paying down debt. With so little debt, there is not much paydown to lean on. The return has to come from running the business better.

Three takeaways

KKR paid a US price for a UK company

The offer was 20.3x 2024 EBITDA, against 11.2x before the bids and 15.8x for UK peers. KKR is betting that Spectris deserves the 22.6x US instrument companies get, and that a future buyer will agree.

This is an operations bet, not a leverage bet

At 70% equity, debt does little of the work. KKR has to grow profit faster than my plan, for example through add-on acquisitions or deeper cost cuts.

The bidding war cost about 3 points of IRR

Going from Advent's £37.63 to £41.75 added £416m to the price. On the same plan that cuts the return from 14.4% to 11.5%.

What I would change

  • The projections are mine. KKR's real plan is private and almost certainly more ambitious.
  • The loan rate (6.5%) is an estimate. Each 1% on £1.5bn is about £15m of interest a year.
  • The diluted share count (101.0m) is derived, not published. It is the only figure that reproduces all six multiples in the offer documents.
  • The balance sheet is simplified: pensions, provisions and tax balances are left out.
  • Peer multiples use a data provider's standard EBITDA at year-ends up to eight months apart, so they are ranges, not precise targets.
  • My equity estimate (£3.48bn) is 5% below the £3.65bn implied by KKR's syndication filing, so the real cheque, and the real return gap, may be larger.
